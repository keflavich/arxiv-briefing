Number 3. Paolo Suin, Patrick Hennebelle and colleagues, paper two in that series, rank two. They extract clumps from the same kiloparsec-scale M H D runs with the HOP algorithm and compare them to the Hi-GAL compact source catalog. Number 4. A. Aberfelds, A. Sanna, L. Moscadelli and colleagues, POETS paper ten, rank twelve. They estimate that "more than 30 % of the whole population is undetected" at typical interferometric sensitivity, and that "90 % of them are distributed within radii of 3000 au only" from the young star. Part two. Deep dives.

Deep dive one. The Sagittarius C outflow.

Sen-Di Bo, Xing Lu and colleagues follow up one of 43 candidate outflows that ALMA found in three C M Z clouds. Sagittarius C F 2 has "a highly collimated lobe extending approximately 0.8 pc in projection". They combine three S I O transitions, J equals 2 to 1, 5 to 4, and 8 to 7, and run both L T E and non-L T E models to get column density, H two density and temperature along the lobe.

The result: low S I O rotational temperatures but higher kinetic temperatures, which they read as "subthermal excitation". Together with the elongated morphology, the position-velocity structure, and the alignment of the redshifted lobe with J W S T infrared knots, these "favor jet-dominated entrainment", although a broader disk wind near the source remains possible. The asymmetry between the two lobes they attribute to dense gas in the path of one lobe and "feedback from a nearby HII region imposing different pressure on its two sides".

What to look at. Figure 1 is the multi-wavelength overview, with blue and red S I O contours on the 1.3 millimeter continuum. Figure 3 gives position-velocity diagrams along the blue lobe for all S I O lines; that's where the jet case lives or dies. Figures 5 and 6 show forward-modeled and RADEX excitation and column density as a function of offset from the protostar.

A skeptical read. Three S I O lines constrain density and temperature only loosely when the excitation is subthermal, so check how degenerate the RADEX solutions in Figure 6 are. The infrared knot alignment is in projection, and the H two region pressure argument is qualitative. Still, a well-collimated jet-driven outflow in the C M Z is a useful data point for whether disk-jet physics changes in the Galactic Center environment.

Deep dive two. What sets the Milky Way's star-formation rate.

Hennebelle, Suin and colleagues run kiloparsec-scale turbulent M H D boxes tuned to solar-neighborhood conditions, with adaptive zooms down to 0.03 parsecs. For Galactic column densities and velocity dispersions, the star-formation rate comes out close to the Milky Way value. Their decomposition: "turbulence provides multiscale support that lowers the SFR by more than an order of magnitude" relative to free-fall collapse, and feedback from ionizing radiation and outflows "reduces the SFR by an additional factor of 3-10". The conclusion is that turbulence plus feedback "limits the star formation efficiency to only a few percent".

What to look at. Figure 5 has the measured sink-particle star-formation rate. Figure 6 compares it with what they call a "Specious SFR from the density PDF", alongside the free-fall model, which shows how badly density-PDF estimates miss. Figure 7 is the Schmidt-Kennicutt relation across scales against several observational sets.

A skeptical read. The feedback factor of 3 to 10 is inferred, since the turbulence-only runs need it to match. And in Figure 7 the authors themselves stress that "for the HI-Gal clumps the SFR is not directly measured but the result of a model", so the clump-scale agreement in paper two partly compares a simulation with a model. The linear mass to star-formation-rate relation in paper two is interesting, but it could partly reflect how HOP defines clumps; Figure 6 of paper two tests HOP parameters and deserves a look. For C M Z work, the open question is how these numbers shift under strong external pressure and shear.

Deep dive three. Silicon-carbon chains around I R C plus 10 2 16.

Tom Millar models linear silicon carbide chains, S i C 3 through S i C 6 being already detected in the carbon-rich A G B envelope, and extends the network to S i C 8, giving the first detailed comparison between observations and models for this family. Using a mix of measured and postulated rate coefficients, the model reproduces observed column densities to within about a factor of four. It predicts that S i C 7 and S i C 8 sit a factor of 2 to 4 below S i C 6, and concludes that detection "will be challenging but that astronomical searches should be undertaken".

A key chemical point: neutral-neutral reactions of the parent S i C 2 with hydrocarbon radicals matter, and neglecting them cuts carbide columns by up to about a factor of six for S i C 6 and S i C 7.

What to look at. Figure 1 shows fractional abundances against radius, so you can see the shell structure. Figure 6 breaks down formation and destruction rates of S i C 6 by reaction channel, which tells you exactly which postulated rates hold up the model. Figure 7 is Model 2, with the S i C 2 plus C n H reactions switched off.

A skeptical read. "Postulated" rate coefficients carry the result, so a factor of four agreement is consistent with the uncertainties rather than a strong test. For your refractory interests, this is the gas-phase side of silicon carbide dust formation, and the same envelope hosts the metal halides, so the radial abundance profiles are worth comparing with your salt work.

Deep dive four. Amorphous silicates in three I ATLAS.

Thomas, Kelley, Harker and colleagues took J W S T MIRI low-resolution spectra of the third interstellar object after perihelion, at 2.21 A U. They see "a prominent 10 micron silicate emission feature with a broad, smooth shape that is distinct from those commonly observed in Solar System comets". Thermal models with amorphous carbon, olivine and pyroxene "strongly favor a pyroxene-dominated, amorphous silicate composition". The "best-fitting crystalline abundance is zero", with an "upper limit of 5.3% on the crystalline fraction". They also flag a feature near 6.83 microns "consistent with aliphatic C-H-bearing material".

The interpretation: the dust "experienced little thermal processing", consistent with earlier reports of unusual volatile and isotopic compositions.

What to look at. Figure 2 is the money plot: the 3I silicate feature shape compared with Solar System comets. Figure 3 is the joint posterior of silicate to carbon ratio against grain size, which shows the degeneracy they mention. Figure 4 walks along that ridge of solutions.

A skeptical read. A single epoch at one heliocentric distance limits what you can say about grain evolution, and the grain size versus composition degeneracy is real. The near-zero crystalline fraction stands out because many Solar System comets show crystalline forsterite peaks. Expect this result to get wide press.

Deep dive five. Starlink telemetry at 137 megahertz.

Yang, Zhao, Guo and colleagues characterize Starlink V H F telemetry, tracking and command transmissions picked up by the 21 Centimeter Array in China. They demodulated and decoded the signals, finding two valid packets at 137.055 megahertz about 100 seconds apart. "A blind comparison with 11 305 active satellites identifies STARLINK-32838" as the transmitter, confirmed by differential Doppler. The satellite was raising its orbit at the time, but re-analysis of earlier E D A 2 detections shows emissions outside orbit raising, so the authors conclude that this interference "should not be considered solely in the context of orbit raising". The stronger packet's estimated flux density is about 9 mega-jansky at 1000 kilometers.

What to look at. Figure 1 shows the dynamic spectra of both events. Figure 2 is the satellite identification, residuals against all active satellites. Figure 8 shows the altitude history of this spacecraft and its launch group.

A skeptical read. Two packets on one night form a thin sample, and the flux agrees with earlier work only at the order-of-magnitude level. The method, decoding the packets to identify the transmitter, is the durable contribution, and it matters for S K A Low.
